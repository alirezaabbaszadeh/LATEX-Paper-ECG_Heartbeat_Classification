\section{Introduction}

An ECG classifier can appear convincing while missing the rhythms for which automated assistance may matter most. Normal beats usually account for most observations in ambulatory recordings, whereas supraventricular ectopic beats (SVEB) and fusion beats are comparatively uncommon and unevenly distributed among patients. Accuracy and weighted averages largely follow the common classes under these conditions. Macro-averaged measures and precision--recall curves expose a different part of the result, but they are still meaningful only when class support and the construction of the test cohort are reported\cite{he2009learning,saito2015precision}. Recent ECG studies have reached the same problem through different clinical tasks, including signal classification, atrial-fibrillation detection, and rhythm discrimination in clinical recordings\cite{mantravadi2024clinet,petmezas2026optimizing,vainoryte2025diagnostic}.

Patient separation is particularly important in beat classification. Beats from one recording share morphology, acquisition conditions, and rhythm history; random allocation of individual beats can therefore place closely related examples on both sides of the training--test split. Holding out entire records gives a more demanding estimate of generalisation, although the estimate may vary sharply when only a few records contain the rare classes\cite{dechazal2004automatic}. This dependence on population and acquisition setting is also evident in studies of automated 12-lead interpretation, mobile lead-I risk assessment, and single-lead QT monitoring\cite{herman2024validation,tuboly2026malignant,doggart2025singlelead}. Results from those tasks are not numerical benchmarks for MIT--BIH beat classification, but they reinforce the need to define exactly what was held out and what each reported metric represents.

The model considered here combines a time--frequency description of individual beats with short-range rhythm context. Wavelet coefficients retain transient ECG morphology at several scales\cite{addison2005wavelet,torrence1998practical,mallat1989wavelet}; related work has used multiresolution representations for ECG denoising and explicit feature selection for rhythm detection\cite{wang2025mrsenet,petmezas2026optimizing}. Context across beats is a separate source of information, as illustrated by analyses based on long-term RR dynamics\cite{pukkila2026chronic}. We used two-dimensional convolutions to encode Morlet scalograms and a Conformer block to model a sequence of three beats\cite{gulati2020conformer}. The Conformer was chosen because its attention and convolutional modules can represent interactions across the short sequence, not because either module has already been shown to solve the minority-class problem.

This study evaluates that architecture on records kept outside model development. Three models sharing the same convolutional beat encoder provide the comparison: a CNN followed by an attention-based sequence encoder, a CNN--LSTM, and a CNN whose three beat embeddings are averaged without a sequence encoder. We report aggregate and class-specific performance together and use the confusion matrix to identify where the proposed model fails. The purpose is to establish what the current experiment supports before broader claims of generalisation or clinical usefulness are made.
