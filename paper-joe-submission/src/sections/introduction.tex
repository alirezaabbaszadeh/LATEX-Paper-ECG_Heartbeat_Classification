\section{Introduction}

An ECG beat classifier is most useful when a tracing departs from the pattern usually seen in that patient. That is also where evaluation becomes difficult. Morphology varies substantially between patients, ambulatory recordings are dominated by normal beats, and supraventricular ectopic (SVEB) and fusion beats may be scarce. A model can therefore improve accuracy while scarcely recognising the rhythms for which automated review is expected to help. Work on imbalanced learning and precision--recall evaluation explains why this discrepancy is easily missed\cite{chawla2002smote,he2009learning,saito2015precision}. The clinically relevant question is not confined to the average score: any gain has to survive a change of patient, and its distribution across rhythm classes has to be visible.

The record partition determines whether an experiment answers that question. Beats from one patient share waveform characteristics, acquisition conditions, and rhythm context. If they are divided randomly between training and test sets, the model is partly tested on morphology it encountered during development. Record-level separation poses the harder problem of recognising beats from patients the model has not seen. Convolutional, recurrent, residual, and patient-adapted systems have demonstrated the value of learned ECG representations\cite{kiranyaz2015personalized,luz2016ecg,hannun2019cardiologist,acharya2017deep}; their reported scores nevertheless describe different tasks when datasets, label taxonomies, segment lengths, or partitioning rules differ\cite{ismailfawaz2019deeplearning}.

Recent papers in the \textit{Journal of Electrocardiology} make this heterogeneity particularly clear. CLINet addresses deep ECG signal classification, whereas other studies use selected ECG features for atrial-fibrillation detection, distinguish atrial fibrillation from competing rhythms with an AI-assisted application, or estimate malignant-arrhythmia risk from mobile lead-I recordings\cite{mantravadi2024clinet,petmezas2026optimizing,vainoryte2025diagnostic,tuboly2026malignant}. These are related uses of ECG-based machine learning, but they have different endpoints and validation boundaries. Treating their headline metrics as entries on one leaderboard would obscure those differences. A useful benchmark must instead state which patients were withheld and show what happened to each class.

Representation introduces a separate choice. Local transient morphology matters within a beat, while the relation between neighbouring beats carries short-range rhythm information. Wavelet transforms provide a multiresolution view of the first component\cite{addison2005wavelet,torrence1998practical,mallat1989wavelet}; recent multiresolution residual-attention work on ECG denoising shows that this remains an active signal-representation problem\cite{wang2025mrsenet}. In the model examined here, convolutional layers encode local patterns in Morlet time--frequency maps and a Conformer integrates the resulting three-beat sequence through self-attention and convolution\cite{gulati2020conformer}. The pairing has a plausible technical rationale. Whether it is useful, however, depends on the errors it produces under patient separation, not on architectural novelty alone.

We evaluated that question on the MIT-BIH Arrhythmia Database using a fixed record-level development/test partition\cite{goldberger2000physiobank,moody2001impact}. The Morlet CNN--Conformer and three implemented alternatives---attention-only, CNN--LSTM, and a feature-engineered classifier---were evaluated on the same seven held-out records. We examined model-level metrics together with class support, precision, recall, F1, AUC, dominant confusion pathways, and post-hoc threshold and calibration diagnostics. This design addresses three linked questions: what the hybrid model improves within the controlled comparison, which rhythms remain outside that gain, and which experiments are warranted by the observed failures.
