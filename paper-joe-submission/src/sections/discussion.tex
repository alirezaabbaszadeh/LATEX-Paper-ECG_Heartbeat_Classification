\section{Discussion}

The main result is a mismatch between model ranking and class coverage. The Morlet CNN--Conformer led the three comparators on accuracy, macro-F1, and weighted-F1 when all four were tested on the same seven records. Its class-wise performance was far narrower: F1 was 0.74 for Normal and 0.57 for VEB, compared with 0.00 for SVEB and 0.01 for Fusion. We therefore regard the model as the strongest comparator in this experiment, but not as an adequate five-class classifier.

Class prevalence accounts for much of the apparent discrepancy between the aggregate measures. Normal and VEB comprised nearly all test beats and dominate the weighted-F1 of 0.68. Macro-F1 falls to 0.26 because it gives equal weight to SVEB, Fusion, and Q. In this dataset, weighted-F1 chiefly describes performance on the two common categories; it should not be read as evidence of consistent performance across rhythms.

Record-level separation makes this limitation more consequential, and also makes it easier to see. None of the seven test records contributed beats to model development, so the final evaluation includes patient morphology and recording context unfamiliar to the classifier. Accuracy across the development folds was $0.44\pm0.16$. We interpret that dispersion as evidence that the composition of a record-level cohort has a substantial effect on the estimate. Seven test records can support the controlled comparison made here, but cannot establish how stable its magnitude would be in another patient sample.

The present comparison does not identify which architectural component produced the aggregate gain. The attention-only and CNN--LSTM models differ from the CNN--Conformer in more than one respect, and the class-specific AUCs do not show uniform dominance. CNN--LSTM, for example, had a slightly higher VEB AUC, while several comparators had higher Fusion or Q AUCs. What the data support is more limited: local convolution followed by Conformer sequence modelling yielded the best combination of the three aggregate metrics within this pipeline. A component-wise ablation is needed before assigning that advantage to convolution, attention, or their interaction.

For model development, the directions of the rare-class errors are more useful than the near-zero F1-scores alone. Ninety-five per cent of SVEB beats were labelled either Normal or VEB, and 90\% of Fusion beats were labelled VEB. We consider the SVEB-to-Normal path the more informative of the two: it is compatible with insufficient use of the timing cues available in a three-beat sequence. The Fusion-to-VEB path is also physiologically plausible if ventricular-like local morphology dominates the representation. These remain hypotheses. Sequence-length, lead, and encoder ablations on the same patient split would show whether either interpretation survives a controlled test.

The threshold analysis narrows the likely explanation. SVEB recall could be raised to 0.90 only with precision of 0.03; Fusion recall of 1.00 coincided with precision of 0.02. A change of operating point would therefore exchange missed minority beats for a very large number of false positives. The result is consistent with substantial overlap between the rare-class scores and those of competing classes. It offers little support for threshold adjustment as a sufficient remedy, although independently selected thresholds were not tested.

Published ECG classifiers provide context, although they cannot supply a numerical benchmark for this experiment. Convolutional, recurrent, residual, and patient-adapted models have been evaluated with different signal durations and validation designs\cite{hannun2019cardiologist,kiranyaz2015personalized,luz2016ecg,acharya2017deep}. The contrast is visible even within the \textit{Journal of Electrocardiology}: CLINet addresses signal-level classification, whereas Pukkila and colleagues analyse long-term RR dynamics for chronic heart failure detection\cite{mantravadi2024clinet,pukkila2026chronic}. Those endpoints place different demands on a representation. The four-model comparison in this study is interpretable because the cohort, labels, and class support are shared.

The confidence results point in the same direction. Normal and VEB had markedly different ECE values, and a single fitted temperature barely changed negative log-likelihood. Certainty and calibrated uncertainty are increasingly reported as separate properties of AI-enabled ECG systems\cite{demolder2024certainty,doggart2025singlelead}. In our analysis, the temperature was fitted on the test cohort and serves only as an internal diagnostic. Its negligible effect makes a global confidence correction an unlikely answer to the class-specific failures.

\subsection*{Limitations}

This study uses one legacy public database and seven held-out records. The record count is too small to represent population, device, or acquisition variability. Rare-class support narrows the inference further: 214 SVEB beats, 383 Fusion beats, and two Q beats leave those estimates sensitive to the rhythms contributed by a few patients. The beat-level bootstrap intervals describe uncertainty among observed beats, not variation across new patient cohorts.

Only the first ECG channel and three adjacent beats were used. The experiment cannot tell whether longer rhythm context, the second lead, or both would alter the dominant error paths. Class-weighted cross-entropy was the sole imbalance treatment in the definitive model; other objectives were not compared under the same protocol. Threshold sweeping and temperature fitting were exploratory analyses of the test predictions and cannot be carried forward as validated operating procedures.

\subsection*{Priorities for further work}

The first follow-up should test the representation implicated by the confusion matrix. With the patient split and training procedure fixed, sequence length and lead count can be varied in a small factorial ablation, followed by removal of the convolutional or attention component. Changes in the SVEB-to-Normal and Fusion-to-VEB paths would be the primary readout. This experiment would distinguish missing input context from a limitation of the encoder more directly than another broad model comparison.

Minority-class learning should then be tested against the current class-weighted loss. Candidate objectives and operating points must be selected entirely within the development records, with class-wise precision--recall performance specified in advance as the main criterion. An improvement confined to weighted-F1 would leave the failure identified here essentially unchanged.

A model fixed after those experiments could proceed to external validation. Contemporary clinical and wearable cohorts would reveal whether Normal/VEB discrimination survives changes in population and acquisition, complementing work on sinus-rhythm prediction and 12-lead diagnosis\cite{attia2019artificial,ribeiro2020automatic}. The validation route should also reflect likely input quality, including digitised ECG and automated 12-lead interpretation workflows for which data-quality dependencies have been documented\cite{herman2024validation,demolder2025digitization}. Prospective clinician-in-the-loop assessment becomes relevant only if rare-class precision--recall performance and independently estimated confidence remain acceptable in those external data.
