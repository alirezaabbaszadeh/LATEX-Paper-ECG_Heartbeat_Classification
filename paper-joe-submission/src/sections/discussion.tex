\section{Discussion}

The central finding of this study is two-sided. First, combining Morlet time--frequency representations with convolutional processing and Conformer blocks produced the strongest model-level performance among the four architectures evaluated on the same subject-separated hold-out. The proposed model reached 0.60 accuracy, 0.26 macro-F1, and 0.68 weighted-F1, compared with 0.27, 0.15, and 0.38 for the strongest competing neural baseline by accuracy. Second, the class-wise analysis shows why these aggregate gains should not be mistaken for uniformly reliable arrhythmia recognition: normal and ventricular ectopic beats were detected substantially better than SVEB and fusion beats, whose F1-scores remained near zero. The scientific value of the result therefore lies not only in the architectural comparison, but also in exposing where the apparent overall gain does and does not translate into rhythm-specific discrimination.

The subject-separated evaluation is consequential for that interpretation. Beats from a single patient share morphology, acquisition characteristics, and rhythm context, so evaluation on unseen records asks a different question from random beat-level partitioning: whether learned representations remain useful when the patient changes. The five-fold development accuracy of $0.44\pm0.16$ and the uneven held-out class performance both show that patient composition materially affects the measured result. Rather than treating this variability as noise around a single headline accuracy, the present analysis makes it part of the evidence. For computerized electrocardiology, such variability is especially relevant because prospective monitoring systems encounter patient-specific morphology and prevalence shifts that cannot be represented by a single pooled metric.

Within the controlled comparisons used here, the results are consistent with a benefit from coupling local convolutional feature extraction with attention-based temporal modelling. Removing the convolutional branch in the attention-only comparator reduced held-out accuracy from 0.60 to 0.27, while replacing the Conformer representation with a CNN--LSTM yielded 0.18. These differences support the chosen hybrid representation for the present Morlet-scalogram pipeline, but they do not establish universal architectural superiority. Class-specific AUCs make that qualification explicit: CNN--LSTM, for example, achieved a slightly higher VEB AUC than the proposed model, and several comparators exceeded the proposed model for fusion or Q-class AUC. The more defensible conclusion is that the proposed configuration achieved the best overall balance of accuracy, macro-F1, and weighted-F1 among the evaluated alternatives, while its advantages remained class dependent.

That class dependence has direct clinical relevance. The model identified normal beats with high precision (0.95) and VEB with recall 0.70 and F1 0.57, suggesting that the representation captures substantial information for the two dominant rhythm groups in this test cohort. In contrast, 71\% of SVEB beats were labelled normal and 90\% of fusion beats were labelled VEB. Post-hoc threshold adjustment could increase minority-class recall only by accepting extremely low precision: the best observed SVEB operating point had precision 0.03 at recall 0.90, and the corresponding fusion analysis had precision 0.02 at recall 1.00. These findings argue against interpreting model probabilities as autonomous diagnostic labels. A more appropriate translational question is whether such outputs can eventually contribute to clinician-supervised prioritisation, burden estimation, or review workflows after substantially stronger minority-class learning, independent operating-point validation, and prospective evaluation.

Comparison with previous deep-learning ECG studies also requires attention to evaluation protocol. Large residual, convolutional, recurrent, and patient-adapted systems have demonstrated the potential of learned ECG representations across ambulatory and wearable settings\cite{hannun2019cardiologist,kiranyaz2015personalized,luz2016ecg,acharya2017deep}. However, differences in datasets, label taxonomies, signal duration, patient partitioning, and clinical endpoints limit direct interpretation of headline metrics across studies. The present work is therefore positioned as a controlled benchmark rather than a claim to outperform the broader literature. Its contribution is narrower but reproducible: under one fixed subject-separated MIT-BIH protocol, the CNN--Conformer configuration improves the principal aggregate metrics relative to three implemented alternatives and makes the resulting class-specific failures visible through confusion, ROC, precision--recall, threshold, and calibration analyses.

Reproducibility is also a practical contribution of the study. The data acquisition, beat extraction, Morlet transformation, TFRecord construction, hyperparameter search, cross-validation, final fitting, and evaluation stages are represented by executable scripts and archived run artifacts. This makes the reported result auditable at a level that is often obscured when preprocessing and evaluation choices are condensed into a single methods paragraph. For future work, the same pipeline can serve as a controlled test bed for changes that specifically target the observed failure modes---for example, alternative objectives for severe imbalance, longer rhythm context, self-supervised pretraining, or multi-lead inputs---without changing the evaluation cohort or silently altering the comparison protocol.

The post-hoc calibration analysis should be interpreted with similar restraint. The normal class was markedly miscalibrated by the 15-bin ECE measure (ECE 0.417 before scaling), whereas VEB showed lower ECE (0.089). A single fitted temperature changed negative log-likelihood only minimally. Importantly, temperature fitting was performed on the held-out test probabilities themselves; the analysis therefore describes confidence behaviour in this cohort but is not an independent calibration validation. A clinically meaningful calibration study would require fitting calibration parameters on a separate development subset and testing them on untouched patients, ideally under external dataset shift.

\subsection*{Limitations}
Several limitations define the boundary of the conclusions. First, the study uses a single legacy public database and a seven-record held-out cohort; this supports controlled benchmarking but not population-level generalisability. Second, the severe class imbalance leaves only 214 SVEB, 383 fusion, and two Q beats in the held-out set, limiting inference for rare categories and making the Q-class metrics essentially descriptive. Third, the pipeline uses the first ECG channel and short three-beat sequences, so additional lead information and longer rhythm context are not exploited. Fourth, class-weighted cross-entropy did not resolve minority-class failure, and alternative imbalance-aware objectives were not compared in the definitive experiment. Fifth, bootstrap confidence intervals quantify uncertainty within the held-out beat set but do not substitute for uncertainty across independent external cohorts. Finally, the calibration and threshold analyses are post-hoc analyses of the same held-out predictions and should not be read as independently validated operating-point or calibration procedures.

\subsection*{Future work}
The next experiments should be driven by the observed failure modes rather than by aggregate accuracy alone. Priority directions include physiologically constrained augmentation or representation learning for SVEB and fusion beats, focal or explicitly cost-sensitive objectives, longer temporal context, and self-supervised pretraining on larger ambulatory ECG corpora. Multi-lead evaluation and external validation on contemporary clinical or wearable datasets are needed to test whether the Normal/VEB discrimination observed here transfers beyond MIT-BIH\cite{attia2019artificial,ribeiro2020automatic}. Calibration parameters and decision thresholds should be selected on development data and evaluated on untouched patients. Finally, prospective clinician-in-the-loop studies should test whether model outputs improve review efficiency or prioritisation without increasing clinically consequential false alarms. These steps would convert the current reproducible benchmark into a stronger test of translational utility while preserving the patient-separated evaluation principles used in this study.
