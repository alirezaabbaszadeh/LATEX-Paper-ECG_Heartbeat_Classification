\textbf{Background:} Patient-to-patient morphological variation and marked class imbalance can make aggregate heartbeat-classification scores look better than performance on rare rhythms. We examined how a Morlet CNN--Conformer behaved on records excluded from model development. \textbf{Methods:} Forty-five non-paced MIT-BIH records were divided at record level. Thirty-eight records were used for development, and seven held-out records containing 15{,}573 beats were reserved for final evaluation. Three adjacent beats were represented as 32-scale Morlet scalograms. The CNN--Conformer was compared with attention-only, CNN--LSTM, and feature-engineered models on the same test cohort. \textbf{Results:} Accuracy was 0.60 (95\% CI 0.59--0.60), macro-F1 was 0.26 (95\% CI 0.26--0.27), and weighted-F1 was 0.68 (95\% CI 0.67--0.68). These were the highest values among the four evaluated architectures; the attention-only comparator reached 0.27, 0.15, and 0.38, respectively. Class-wise results were much less uniform. F1 was 0.74 for Normal and 0.57 for ventricular ectopic beats, but 0.00 for SVEB and 0.01 for Fusion. Post-hoc threshold sweeps increased minority-class recall only at very low precision. \textbf{Conclusions:} The CNN--Conformer provided the strongest aggregate comparator under this subject-separated protocol, although its advantage was confined largely to Normal and ventricular ectopic beats. Rare-rhythm recognition remains unresolved and requires targeted work on temporal context and minority-class learning before external validation.
