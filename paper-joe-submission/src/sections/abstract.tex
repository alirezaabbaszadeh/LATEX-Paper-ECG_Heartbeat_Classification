\textbf{Background:} Aggregate scores can give a misleading account of heartbeat classification when morphology varies between patients and rare rhythms contribute little to the average. We tested whether a Morlet CNN--Conformer could improve performance on unseen records while making the limits of that improvement explicit. \textbf{Methods:} Forty-five non-paced MIT-BIH records were divided at record level. Thirty-eight records were used for development; seven held-out records containing 15{,}573 beats were reserved for final evaluation. Three adjacent beats were represented as 32-scale Morlet scalograms. On the same test cohort, the CNN--Conformer was compared with attention-only, CNN--LSTM, and feature-engineered baselines. \textbf{Results:} The CNN--Conformer achieved 0.60 accuracy (95\% CI 0.59--0.60), 0.26 macro-F1 (95\% CI 0.26--0.27), and 0.68 weighted-F1 (95\% CI 0.67--0.68), leading the four evaluated architectures on all three aggregate metrics. The corresponding values for the attention-only comparator were 0.27, 0.15, and 0.38. The class report changed how that lead should be read: Normal and ventricular ectopic beats reached F1-scores of 0.74 and 0.57, whereas SVEB and Fusion reached 0.00 and 0.01. Post-hoc threshold sweeps raised minority-class recall only at very low precision. \textbf{Conclusions:} Within this subject-separated benchmark, the hybrid model improved aggregate performance without solving rare-rhythm recognition. The fixed evaluation boundary, archived predictions, and class-wise diagnostics establish a reproducible starting point for imbalance-aware modelling and external validation.
