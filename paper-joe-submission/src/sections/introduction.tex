\section{Introduction}

Automated heartbeat classification has an awkward evaluation problem: its easiest class is usually its largest. Normal beats dominate most ambulatory recordings, whereas supraventricular ectopic (SVEB) and fusion beats may be sparse and vary appreciably between patients. Accuracy and weighted summaries can consequently improve even when those rare rhythms are seldom identified. This behaviour is well described in the class-imbalance literature, and it explains the particular value of precision--recall analysis when prevalence is low\cite{chawla2002smote,he2009learning,saito2015precision}. For arrhythmia research, a model-level score is informative only when the patient split and the distribution of errors across classes are also known.

Patient separation is central to that interpretation. Beats from the same person share waveform characteristics, acquisition conditions, and rhythm context; assigning them randomly to both training and test sets allows familiar patient morphology to cross the evaluation boundary. A record-level split asks the harder question of how the classifier responds to patients absent from development. Convolutional, recurrent, residual, and patient-adapted systems have established the value of learned ECG representations\cite{kiranyaz2015personalized,luz2016ecg,hannun2019cardiologist,acharya2017deep}. Comparisons among them remain sensitive to dataset, label taxonomy, segment duration, and, especially, partitioning strategy\cite{ismailfawaz2019deeplearning}.

The range of recent work in the \textit{Journal of Electrocardiology} illustrates the problem. CLINet addresses deep ECG signal classification\cite{mantravadi2024clinet}, while Petmezas and colleagues select ECG features for atrial-fibrillation detection\cite{petmezas2026optimizing}. Vainoryt{\.e} and colleagues evaluate AI-assisted differentiation of atrial fibrillation from other rhythms\cite{vainoryte2025diagnostic}; Tuboly and colleagues estimate malignant-arrhythmia risk from mobile lead-I recordings\cite{tuboly2026malignant}. All concern machine learning from ECG, but their endpoints and validation boundaries are different. Their headline values cannot be transferred directly to five-class beat classification. Within a benchmark, the more useful comparison is between models tested on the same patients and judged with the same class definitions.

The representation must accommodate information on two time scales. Transient morphology is local to a beat, while the relation between neighbouring beats supplies short-range rhythm context. Wavelet transforms offer a multiresolution description of the former\cite{addison2005wavelet,torrence1998practical,mallat1989wavelet}, and recent residual-attention work on ECG denoising shows continued interest in multiresolution signal representations\cite{wang2025mrsenet}. In the present model, convolutions encode Morlet time--frequency maps and a Conformer combines self-attention with convolution across a three-beat sequence\cite{gulati2020conformer}. This pairing is technically plausible, but its value depends on the class errors observed after patient separation.

We studied the Morlet CNN--Conformer on the MIT-BIH Arrhythmia Database using a fixed record-level development/test partition\cite{goldberger2000physiobank,moody2001impact}. Three implemented alternatives---attention-only, CNN--LSTM, and a feature-engineered classifier---were tested on the same seven held-out records. Model-level comparisons were read alongside class support, precision, recall, F1, AUC, dominant confusion pathways, and post-hoc threshold and calibration analyses. The aim was to determine where the hybrid model improved on the available comparators, where it failed, and which follow-up experiments those failures justify.
