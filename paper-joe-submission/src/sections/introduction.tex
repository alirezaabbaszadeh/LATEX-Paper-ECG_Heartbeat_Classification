\section{Introduction}
Automated ECG analysis is most valuable when it can recognise a meaningful rhythm change without mistaking normal patient-to-patient variation for disease. Heartbeat classification makes that difficult. Ambulatory ECG recordings contain substantial differences in morphology between patients, while their class distribution is usually dominated by normal beats. Supraventricular ectopic and fusion beats may be comparatively scarce. In that setting, an encouraging overall score can hide poor recognition of the very rhythms for which automated review is expected to help\cite{chawla2002smote,he2009learning,saito2015precision}. The practical question is therefore broader than whether a classifier improves accuracy: it is whether that improvement persists across patients and whether the remaining errors are concentrated in clinically relevant minority rhythms.

How the data are divided is central to answering that question. Beats recorded from the same patient share morphology, acquisition characteristics, and rhythm context. Randomly distributing such beats across training and test sets can therefore measure a different, and often easier, problem than testing on records that were excluded from model development. Convolutional, recurrent, residual, and patient-adapted approaches have all shown the potential of learned ECG representations\cite{kiranyaz2015personalized,luz2016ecg,hannun2019cardiologist,acharya2017deep}, but headline results are difficult to compare when datasets, label taxonomies, segment duration, and partitioning strategy differ\cite{ismailfawaz2019deeplearning}. Recent work in the \textit{Journal of Electrocardiology} extends this literature from hybrid deep ECG classification to feature-selected atrial-fibrillation detection, AI-assisted differentiation of atrial fibrillation from competing rhythms, and machine-learning risk assessment from mobile lead-I recordings\cite{mantravadi2024clinet,petmezas2026optimizing,vainoryte2025diagnostic,tuboly2026malignant}. These studies are relevant comparators, but their differing endpoints and validation designs preclude direct ranking by headline scores. For a benchmark to be informative, the evaluation boundary and the class-specific behaviour need to be as visible as the final aggregate metric.

The representation itself also has to capture information at more than one scale. Beat morphology contains local transient structure, while the relationship between neighbouring beats adds short-term rhythm context. Wavelet transforms provide a multiresolution view of ECG morphology\cite{addison2005wavelet,torrence1998practical,mallat1989wavelet}. Signal quality remains part of this representation problem; multiresolution residual-attention denoising has recently been evaluated for ECG restoration\cite{wang2025mrsenet}. Convolutional layers can emphasise local patterns in those time--frequency maps, whereas self-attention can integrate information across the short sequence. Conformer encoders combine these operations through convolution, multi-head self-attention, and feed-forward modules\cite{gulati2020conformer}. This makes a Morlet CNN--Conformer a reasonable model to test, but architectural novelty alone is not sufficient evidence of value. Any aggregate gain must also be examined against the model's behaviour on underrepresented rhythms.

We therefore evaluated a Morlet CNN--Conformer on the MIT-BIH Arrhythmia Database using a fixed record-level development/test partition that preserves subject separation at final evaluation\cite{goldberger2000physiobank,moody2001impact}. The study was designed to make both the gain and its limits auditable. The same held-out cohort was used to compare the proposed configuration with attention-only, CNN--LSTM, and feature-engineered alternatives, while class support, precision, recall, F1, AUC, dominant confusion pathways, and post-hoc threshold and calibration diagnostics were examined alongside the aggregate scores. The aim was not to claim universal architectural superiority, but to determine what the hybrid representation improves under a controlled protocol, where it still fails, and what those failures imply for future clinician-supervised evaluation.
