\section{Discussion}

This study produced a mixed but informative result. Under the fixed subject-separated protocol, the Morlet CNN--Conformer had the strongest aggregate score profile among the four architectures evaluated. At the same time, that advantage did not extend evenly across rhythm classes. Normal and ventricular ectopic beats accounted for most of the realised classification performance, whereas SVEB and fusion beats remained difficult to recognise. The important finding is therefore not simply that the proposed model improved the aggregate metrics. It is that the same evaluation also shows where that improvement stops.

Subject separation matters to that interpretation. Beats from one patient are not independent examples of morphology: they share recording conditions, waveform characteristics, and rhythm context. Testing on records excluded from development asks whether the representation remains useful when the patient changes, rather than whether it can recognise more beats from patients it has already seen. The five-fold development accuracy of $0.44\pm0.16$ reinforces this point by showing substantial variation across record partitions. In an ECG setting, that variation should not be dismissed as background noise; it is part of the evidence about how sensitive the measured result is to patient composition.

The controlled model comparison supports the selected hybrid configuration for this Morlet-scalogram pipeline, but it does not identify a single architectural component as the cause of the gain. The attention-only and CNN--LSTM alternatives performed less well on the principal aggregate metrics, yet these comparators are not a complete one-factor ablation. Class-specific AUCs also show that the advantage is not universal: CNN--LSTM, for example, achieved a slightly higher VEB AUC, and several alternatives exceeded the proposed model for fusion or Q-class AUC. The most defensible interpretation is therefore narrower and more useful: among the implemented alternatives, the CNN--Conformer provided the best overall balance of accuracy, macro-F1, and weighted-F1, while its class-specific strengths remained uneven.

That unevenness is the main translational concern. Normal beats were identified with high precision (0.95), and VEB reached recall 0.70 with F1 0.57. In contrast, 71\% of SVEB beats were labelled normal and 90\% of fusion beats were labelled VEB. Changing the operating threshold did not remove this pattern. The best observed post-hoc SVEB threshold produced precision 0.03 at recall 0.90, while the corresponding fusion analysis produced precision 0.02 at recall 1.00. In other words, recall could be raised only by accepting a very large false-positive burden. This is why the model should not be read as an autonomous arrhythmia detector. A more relevant next question is whether, after substantially better minority-class learning and independent validation, such outputs could support clinician-supervised review rather than replace it.

These results also illustrate why direct comparison with published ECG classifiers requires care. Deep convolutional, recurrent, residual, and patient-adapted systems have demonstrated the potential of learned ECG representations in ambulatory and wearable settings\cite{hannun2019cardiologist,kiranyaz2015personalized,luz2016ecg,acharya2017deep}. Recent \textit{Journal of Electrocardiology} examples range from signal-level deep classification to long-term RR-dynamics classification, further illustrating that model performance depends on the endpoint and time scale\cite{mantravadi2024clinet,pukkila2026chronic}. However, differences in datasets, label definitions, signal duration, patient partitioning, and clinical endpoints make headline metrics difficult to compare at face value. We therefore position the present work as a controlled benchmark rather than a claim to outperform the broader literature. Its contribution is the combination of a fixed subject-separated protocol, implemented comparator models, and a class-wise failure analysis that makes the limitations visible rather than allowing them to disappear inside a single summary score.

Reproducibility strengthens that contribution. The data acquisition, beat extraction, Morlet transformation, TFRecord construction, hyperparameter search, cross-validation, final fitting, and evaluation stages are represented by executable scripts and archived run artifacts. The stored predictions also allow the reported confusion patterns, threshold sweeps, and calibration diagnostics to be inspected directly. This creates a useful baseline for future experiments aimed specifically at the observed bottleneck, such as alternative imbalance-aware objectives, longer rhythm context, self-supervised pretraining, or multi-lead inputs, without silently changing the evaluation cohort.

The calibration analysis should be read with the same caution as the threshold sweep. Normal beats showed a 15-bin ECE of 0.417 before scaling, whereas VEB showed 0.089, and a single fitted temperature changed negative log-likelihood only minimally. Because the temperature parameter was fitted on the held-out test probabilities themselves, this analysis describes confidence behaviour in the current cohort; it is not an independent validation of calibration. A clinically meaningful calibration study would require the calibration parameters to be selected on development data and then tested on untouched patients, ideally under external dataset shift.

\subsection*{Limitations}
Several limitations set clear boundaries on the conclusions. The study uses one legacy public database and a seven-record held-out cohort, which is suitable for controlled benchmarking but not for population-level generalisation. The test set is also strongly imbalanced, with 214 SVEB beats, 383 fusion beats, and only two Q beats; inference for rare categories is therefore limited, and the Q-class metrics are essentially descriptive. Only the first ECG channel and short three-beat sequences are used, so additional lead information and longer rhythm context are not represented. Class-weighted cross-entropy did not resolve the minority-class failure, and alternative imbalance-aware objectives were not compared in the definitive experiment. Bootstrap intervals quantify uncertainty within the held-out beat set but do not replace uncertainty across independent cohorts. Finally, the threshold and calibration analyses are post-hoc analyses of the same held-out predictions and should not be interpreted as independently validated operating procedures.

\subsection*{Future work}
The next experiments should be driven by the observed failure modes rather than by aggregate accuracy alone. Priority directions include physiologically constrained augmentation or representation learning for SVEB and fusion beats, focal or explicitly cost-sensitive objectives, longer temporal context, and self-supervised pretraining on larger ambulatory ECG corpora. Multi-lead evaluation and external validation on contemporary clinical or wearable datasets are needed to determine whether the Normal/VEB discrimination observed here transfers beyond MIT-BIH\cite{attia2019artificial,ribeiro2020automatic}. External validation should also test acquisition and preprocessing paths likely to appear in practice, including photographed or digitised ECGs and automated 12-lead interpretation systems, whose performance and data-quality dependencies have been examined in recent studies\cite{herman2024validation,demolder2025digitization}. Calibration parameters and decision thresholds should be selected on development data and evaluated on untouched patients. Prospective clinician-in-the-loop studies would then be needed to determine whether the outputs improve review efficiency or prioritisation without creating an unacceptable false-alarm burden.
